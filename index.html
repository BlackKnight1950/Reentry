The money

Nearly two-thirds of the federal money is spoken for before the first hire.

Federal money arrives on every claim. The share on services CCH already provides, which HFS's draft labels existing, goes back into the program. The share on new services is what CCH keeps to pay for the program. CCH sends the state share to HFS through an IGT and the model does not count it as relief.

$K shown in $M. Claims are priced at the Medicaid fee schedule, not CCH cost. Federal match is blended: 70% Cermak, 52.91% JTDC (illustrative; actual depends on the expansion mix).

Planning and Implementation Fund (STC 4.12)

The Planning and Implementation Fund turns Year 1 positive. It is also the least certain number in the model.

CCH PMOE's premise is that the Planning and Implementation Fund covers the Year 1 cost of all new hires plus one-time technology. The STC covers recruiting, hiring, onboarding and training and "the applicable administrative match." It does not say Year 1 salary is covered or what the match rate is.

What it is

The Planning and Implementation Fund is federal money, paid at the administrative match, that STC 4.12 sets aside to get reentry services running: planning, technology, and recruiting, hiring, onboarding and training of staff. It is capped each year for the whole state, shared by every qualified applicant, and each applicant's share is set by need. The STC calls it the Reentry Demonstration Initiative Planning and Implementation Program.

The point to bring to HFS: this is CCH's funding source for start-up. It is the STC provision that pays for what the cost file leaves out, which is recruiting, onboarding and training, plus the one-time technology. The model assumes a Year 1 claim of $1.79M.

What CCH would claim in Year 1

Three ways the recovery could shrink

  • Match rate. The STC pays "the applicable administrative match" and gives no rate. At a half match, the two-year result moves from −$0.41M to −$1.30M.
  • Double funding. Case managers and CHWs bill services at the fee schedule. STC 4.12 requires cost allocation to Medicaid, so HFS may not pay both.
  • Size of the claim. $1.79M is 55% of the statewide DY10 cap of $3.23M, shared by all applicants. Unspent amounts roll over (STC 4.12, Table 1).
Without the Planning and Implementation Fund, CCH covers $2.20M over two years instead of $0.41M. Year 2 does not depend on it either way.

Decisions

Three decisions from HFS change the picture.

Each one is a reading of the STC that HFS has not yet settled. The impacts below come from the workbook formulas at 100% billing.

1

Label expanded case management as new

HFS's draft labels 100% of case management existing. STC 4.11 treats federal dollars on the increase from expanded or enhanced services as new, which CCH keeps. The 8 coordinators are the expansion.

About 47% labeled new makes kept money cover the coordinators. About 50% covers the whole Year 2 shortfall.

+$0.84Mover two years for each 25 points relabeled, at 100% billing
2

Confirm what the Planning and Implementation Fund pays for

Confirm that Year 1 salary for new hires and one-time technology qualify, at what match rate, and that staff who also bill services are not excluded.

The claim is 55% of the statewide DY10 cap, so HFS also decides whether CCH can receive that share.

$1.79Mof the two-year result rests on this answer
3

Say what reinvestment may pay for

STC 4.11(a)(iii) lists covering new, enhanced or expanded pre-release services as an allowable reinvestment. The model treats every program cost as CCH's own to cover.

The answer decides whether any of the $9.96M can carry program cost, and how much of the gap CCH carries.

Not modeledno reinvestment dollars are applied to program cost
How the reinvestment plan connects: the companion plan commits $8.41M to programs and holds back $1.55M of the pool. If HFS labels 46% of case management new, that $1.55M is no longer owed for reinvestment, and CCH keeps it as new spend to apply to program cost. At 100% billing the two-year result with the Planning and Implementation Fund moves from −$0.41M to $1.14M (−$0.64M without the fund), and Year 2 moves from −$1.14M to −$0.10M. If HFS does not agree, the $1.55M stays obligated and needs a use HFS accepts. Try it with the "Release the $1.55M" setting in the stress test. Year by year the timing differs, because the reinvestment obligation is measured over the demonstration.
What CCH does on its side: enter its own count of qualifying exits, confirm existing case management staff and hours, and replace the 1,500 billable-hour assumption with the CCH productivity standard. HFS also needs to confirm readiness before CCH can claim (STC 4.9), and every provider must be enrolled in Medicaid (STC 4.6.a).
One trade-off to know: relabeling case management as new moves the same federal dollars out of the reinvestment amount. At 50% relabeled, the amount to reinvest falls by about $1.69M, from $9.96M to about $8.27M (derived from the model's case management federal share).
Perspective

Same hiring positions, three ways to fund them.

This is PMOE's opinion on the risk, told as an analogy. CCH has very few hiring positions (PIDs) to give out, and clinical and administrative teams have proposals for programs, innovations and grants that want them. The cost file lists 27 positions for reentry, $4.39M over two years with benefits. The grant and the service line below are examples chosen for the comparison, not CCH figures.

Reentry waiver

This proposal

What pays for the positions. Fee-for-service billing after the care is delivered, plus the Planning and Implementation Fund for Year 1 hires and technology if HFS agrees.

What it depends on. Reaching each person at Cermak or JTDC and documenting the service. People have no set release date and are often released before health services knows.

If it falls short. CCH covers it. The two-year result is −$0.41M at 100% billing and −$1.24M at 70%, both with the Fund (−$2.20M at 100% without it).

A grant

For example, Ryan White

What pays for the positions. An award that covers the cost of implementation and is set before anyone is hired.

What it depends on. Meeting the grant's terms and reporting. It is not tied to billing, so it does not depend on finding patients one at a time.

If it falls short. No billing shortfall for CCH, on the premise that the award covers the cost. Confirm the terms of any specific grant.

A service line

For example, the Heart Institute

What pays for the positions. Patient revenue in a market CCH can size before it hires.

What it depends on. Demand and payer mix, which a market analysis can test in advance.

If it falls short. The premise is that the market analysis shows the line covers implementation and earns an 8% profit. That analysis is not in these workbooks.

The question the CEO can fairly ask. The same 27 positions could go to a grant that is funded before anyone is hired, or to a service line with a sized market and a margin. Why give them to a program that only pays back if CCH knows who is leaving Cermak and JTDC, and when, in time to deliver and bill the service?
PMOE's read. As written, the reentry request is the weakest of the three on risk. The result is negative even if every release is captured: −$0.41M over two years, with $1.14M short in Year 2 once the Fund ends. Every missed release makes it worse. A grant with funding set in advance, or a service line with a sized margin, asks the CEO to carry far less.
What would change this view. HFS agreeing to the decisions above: at 46% of case management labeled new, the two-year result is +$1.14M. A dependable release notice from Cermak and JTDC to health services, so billing moves toward the 100% the model assumes. A CCH count of real releases to replace Milliman's.

Program 1 · Recovery Bridge: opioid treatment continuity after release

$5.96Mtwo years

What it is

Recovery Bridge keeps people with opioid use disorder on medication, and in contact with someone who knows them, through the weeks after release from Cermak or JTDC. Medication started or continued in custody is paid for by the Medicaid benefit, so this program does not fund it. It funds what the benefit does not. The goal is that people leave custody with a named person and a booked first visit, and are on medication in the community within days, not weeks.

Who it serves

People released from Cermak with opioid use disorder: about 1,896 a year at the 78% planning share. Milliman assumes no medication treatment at JTDC, so JTDC is not sized here.

What it provides

  • MOUD handoff protocols and clinical oversight. A clinical lead keeps medication going across the release date: bridge prescriptions, opioid treatment program handoffs and restart after a missed dose.
  • Peer recovery coaches. A peer with lived experience meets the person at release, goes with them to the first dose or appointment, and stays in contact.
  • Release-day overdose prevention. A two-dose naloxone kit, overdose education and first-appointment details.
  • First-week rides to the first dose and treatment visits, so a missed ride does not become a missed dose.
  • Reserved capacity at community opioid treatment partners: justice-trained counselors and prescribers, first-visit slots and seven-day access, with a subsidy that steps down as partners' Medicaid billing takes over.

Staffing

36.1 FTE. 19 are CCH employees (1 nurse practitioner clinical lead, 16 peer recovery coaches, 2 coach supervisors). 17.06 are at 6 partner sites (11.38 counselors, 5.69 prescribers), subsidized at 100% in Year 1 and 75% in Year 2.

How we would know it works

Proposed targets, to negotiate with HFS: 80% of people contacted by a peer within 24 hours, 60% starting community treatment within 30 days, 50% still on medication at 90 days. Overdose deaths are tracked against a baseline, with no number promised.

Does not pay for: MOUD itself or the 30-day supply at release (benefit, STC 4.4); post-release care coordination (MCOs); laptops, phones and the data platform (the technology request below).

What it is

Reentry Care Access buys appointment supply for the people the justice system releases, because coverage is not care. It reserves same-week behavioral health appointments with justice-trained community partners, paid through a start-up subsidy that steps down as the partners' Medicaid billing takes over, and gives each partner a named clinician as the referral contact. As an optional component, it adds a reentry clinic care team in CCH's own clinics. Visits are billed to Medicaid and are not funded here. The program pays for the capacity, the training and the supplies that make the appointments available.

Who it serves

People released from Cermak and JTDC who need behavioral health or primary care and are not served through Program 1. The program is not volume-based: no sourced share of exits with these needs was found, so partner clinician counts and the clinic team are design choices, and CCH must supply the need.

What it provides

  • Reserved same-week appointments with a justice-trained clinician at each partner site, for mental health and non-opioid substance use needs, plus part-time psychiatric prescriber time.
  • Declining start-up subsidy: 100% of partner clinician cost in Year 1, 75% in Year 2, so the program does not pay for visits Medicaid already pays.
  • Reentry clinic care team (optional): a protected panel in CCH clinics with a community health worker who has lived experience of incarceration and an RN for chronic condition and medication management.
  • Training for partner and clinic staff, including training that keeps the community health worker role inside the clinic team.

Staffing

11.5 FTE. 4 are CCH employees on the optional clinic care team (3 community health workers and 1 RN). 7.5 are at partner sites (6 clinicians and 1.5 FTE of psychiatric prescriber time), subsidized at 100% in Year 1 and 75% in Year 2.

How we would know it works

Proposed targets: a median of 7 days from release to first appointment, and 80% of reserved slots filled. For the clinic component, 60% with a primary care visit within 14 days and fewer emergency department visits than a matched group.

Does not pay for: billable visits (Medicaid); post-release care coordination (MCOs); laptops and phones (the technology request). The community health worker role stays inside the clinic team.

Questions we expect

The hard questions, answered with the workbook's own numbers.

Will fewer ED visits pay for this?

No, and the proposal does not claim it. At $530 per ED visit (HCUP 2017), covering Year 2 cost takes 9,912 avoided visits, 0.82 per person released.

Applying the Transitions Clinic trial effect to everyone released, an upper bound at a placeholder baseline of one ED visit per person, covers about 18% to 62% of Year 2 cost. Savings accrue mostly to Medicaid payers, and fewer visits may reduce CCH hospital revenue.

The case is that the pool must be spent on allowable uses anyway, and these are the best-supported uses.

Isn't this care coordination, which the MCOs own?

HFS's approved Implementation Plan puts post-release care coordination with the MCOs. This portfolio funds treatment continuity and treatment capacity, not case management. The overlap test below shows who does what. The exact section of the plan is to be confirmed before quoting it to HFS.

Peer coaching may still draw a billing-overlap question with Medicaid peer services. HFS is asked to confirm.

What happens after June 30, 2029?

Recurring costs end or fall back on CCH unless the demonstration is extended. The Year 2 run-rate is $5.25M a year. The partner subsidy steps down from 100% to 75% so partners' Medicaid billing takes over, but staff CCH employs would need another source.

Why is Program 2 smaller?

The evidence is weaker and no sourced volume exists for it. No trial tested capacity itself. The Transitions Clinic trial found fewer ED visits but no difference in primary care visits, hospitalization or return to jail, and CrimeSolutions rates it Ineffective (one study). The clinic team is optional and has its own switch.

If HFS approves less than the full portfolio, Tier 1 is funded first, then Tier 2. The shared core is required with any funded program.
